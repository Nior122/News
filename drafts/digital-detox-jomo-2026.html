What Is JOMO? The Joy of Missing Out, Explained (and How to Practice It)

What Is JOMO?

JOMO stands for the joy of missing out: the contentment you feel when you skip an event, trend, or online conversation on purpose and are glad you did. It is the opposite of FOMO, the fear of missing out. JOMO is a choice, not a rule against fun.

Dictionaries treat it as informal slang. Merriam-Webster describes it as the joy of not attending events you were invited to, and Dictionary.com frames it as contentment with your own pursuits without worrying about what others are doing. The key idea is choice: missing out by accident, or because you were left out, is a different experience.

A few everyday examples of JOMO:

  • Staying in on a Saturday night and not checking what everyone else is posting.
  • Muting a busy group chat and catching up when it suits you.
  • Leaving a gathering early, without guilt, because you are ready to go home.

Where the Term JOMO Came From

Merriam-Webster lists 2012 as the first known use of JOMO in this sense and credits no one. Most explainers, including Khaleej Times, credit blogger and tech entrepreneur Anil Dash, who published a short post titled “JOMO!” on July 19, 2012. He opened by pointing to his friend Caterina Fake's writing on FOMO, then described choosing to stay home for his baby's bath and bedtime over almost anywhere else, and named that feeling the joy of missing out.

There is a wrinkle. Word Spy, which catalogs new words, lists an earlier citation: a tweet by Amanda Gillies dated February 5, 2012. So Dash is best described as the person who popularized JOMO and gave it a clear meaning, not necessarily the first to type it.

FOMO has a longer history. Dictionary.com dates its popularization to 2004, and in 2013 Andrew Przybylski and colleagues published a paper that defined it for research and built a scale to measure it.

JOMO vs FOMO

Researchers define FOMO as a pervasive worry that others are having rewarding experiences you are missing, paired with a desire to stay continually connected. Przybylski's team also showed that FOMO motivates social media use. JOMO flips the stance: you accept the gap and enjoy what you chose instead.

Feeling a pang of FOMO does not mean you are failing at JOMO. What matters is what you do next: scroll anxiously, or remember why you chose to stay in. Our look at how short-form video algorithms affect attention covers why feeds make the first reaction so easy.

JOMO vs Digital Detox vs Minimalism

These ideas overlap, but they are not the same thing.

  • JOMO is a mindset: finding satisfaction in what you chose not to do, for one evening or as a long-term habit.
  • A digital detox is a behavior: a set period of cutting back on devices or platforms, from a screen-free evening to a month off social media. JOMO often makes a detox feel like relief rather than punishment. For what a longer break involves, read our piece on the mental benefits of quitting social media.
  • Minimalism is a wider approach to life: owning, doing, and committing to less so that what remains matters more.

JOMO does not require you to be against technology. You can enjoy streaming or a lively group chat and still feel JOMO about the fifty other things you skipped. In short, a detox is something you do, minimalism is how you set up your life, and JOMO is how you feel about what you leave out.

What Are the Benefits of JOMO? What the Research Supports

Research on JOMO itself is young, so it helps to separate what has been studied directly from what is only related.

Directly about JOMO. A 2025 paper in Psychological Reports by Kantar and colleagues, which also validated a JOMO scale, reports that higher JOMO was associated with lower social media addiction, partly through lower loneliness and psychological distress. That is an association, not proof that JOMO causes those outcomes.

Related: cutting back. In a 2018 University of Pennsylvania experiment, 143 undergraduates limited Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat to 10 minutes per platform per day for three weeks. Compared with a control group, they reported less loneliness and depression, with the largest gains among students who were more depressed at the start. The researchers suggested that closer attention to your own use may be part of the reason. The sample was small, made up of students, and the study was short.

The wider picture is modest. An Oxford analysis of large datasets found that technology use explained at most 0.4% of the variation in adolescent wellbeing, and a follow-up study of more than 17,000 teenagers found little clear-cut evidence that screen time, even shortly before bed, lowers wellbeing. A 2024 umbrella review of 102 meta-analyses in Nature Human Behaviour found small-to-moderate associations and rated most of those meta-analyses at medium-to-high risk of bias. Its authors recommend weighing specific types of screen use rather than treating all screen time alike.

Most of this work involves teenagers and students, so it may not transfer neatly to every adult. Online claims that JOMO lowers stress hormones or fixes sleep did not have solid support in the sources we reviewed, so we do not repeat them.

When JOMO Isn't Healthy

Real JOMO is a choice you feel good about. It can drift into avoidance, which looks similar from the outside.

Signs it may be slipping:

  • You feel relief, but not joy, and the relief is mostly about escaping something, such as conflict or anxiety.
  • You are skipping people, not just events, including the friends you care about most.
  • Your circle is shrinking and you cannot remember deciding that.
  • Staying in has become automatic, and you no longer consider going.

A simple test: if you felt calm and well, would you still say no? If yes, that is probably JOMO. If the honest answer is "no, I would go," something else, such as anxiety, low mood, or burnout, may be making the choice for you.

Isolation has real stakes. The 2023 U.S. Surgeon General advisory describes loneliness and social isolation as independent risk factors for conditions including cardiovascular disease, dementia, and depression. JOMO should free up time for the people and things you care about, not cut you off from them.

If anxiety or low mood is persistent, or you are pulling away from people and cannot seem to stop, consider talking with a doctor, therapist, or other qualified professional. JOMO is not a substitute for care.

How to Practice JOMO: 7 Practical Ways

  1. Decide your yeses first. Write down the three to five things that matter most this month: people, projects, rest. Anything that competes with them is a candidate for a no.
  2. Learn a short, kind no. “Thanks for thinking of me, I'm going to sit this one out” is enough. You do not owe an elaborate excuse, and you can add an alternative: “Coffee next week?”
  3. Silence what does not need to interrupt you. Turn off badges, banners, and sounds for everything except calls and messages from people who genuinely need to reach you.
  4. Put a boundary around your feeds. Choose a daily limit or time window for social apps. The Penn experiment used 10 minutes per platform per day; start with a limit you can keep and adjust from there.
  5. Protect one open evening a week. Leave it blank on purpose, with no plans and no obligations, and see what you do with it.
  6. Swap an endless feed for something finite. Choose a paperback, a puzzle, a walk, or a radio show. Single-purpose devices can help too; see our pieces on why people want “dumb tech” again and the comeback of e-ink gadgets.
  7. Check how it felt the next day. After you skip something, ask yourself honestly whether you feel relief, joy, or regret. Keep what feels good and adjust what does not. This is also how you tell JOMO from avoidance.

JOMO at Work and With Friends

At work, JOMO is not slacking; it is choosing where your attention goes. Set response windows for email and chat, skip optional meetings where you have no role and ask for the notes, mute channels you do not need, and tell your team how to reach you for something urgent. Some roles, such as on-call or customer support, require availability, so agree on boundaries with your manager. To decide what to drop in the first place, see why traditional to-do lists fail.

With friends, be honest rather than vague. Decline the event, not the person, and suggest another time. Going silent and leaving people guessing is avoidance, not JOMO. For company online without the firehose, smaller, quieter spaces can help; see our piece on tiny internet communities.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does JOMO mean?

JOMO means “joy of missing out”: the contentment of skipping something on purpose and being glad you did. Merriam-Webster classes it as informal and dates its first known use to 2012.

Who coined JOMO?

Anil Dash is most often credited, thanks to his July 19, 2012 blog post “JOMO!” Merriam-Webster names no originator and Word Spy lists an earlier February 2012 tweet, so “popularized by Dash” is the safest wording.

JOMO vs FOMO: what is the difference?

FOMO is anxiety that others are having rewarding experiences without you. JOMO is contentment with what you chose to skip. FOMO pushes toward constant connection; JOMO asks what you actually want to spend time on.

Is JOMO just being antisocial?

No, not when it is working. JOMO means choosing which plans deserve your time, often so you can show up better for the ones that matter. It becomes a problem when it turns into avoidance or isolation.

How do I practice JOMO?

Decide your priorities first, learn one short and kind way to decline, silence nonessential notifications, set a boundary around feeds, and protect one unplanned evening a week. The seven ways above give the full plan.

JOMO vs digital detox vs minimalism: which is which?

JOMO is a mindset about enjoying what you leave out. A digital detox is a time-limited break from devices or platforms. Minimalism is a broader approach to owning and doing less. They work well together, but none requires the others.

Can JOMO apply at work?

Yes: use response windows instead of instant replies, skip optional meetings that do not need you, and mute low-value channels. Check expectations with your manager, especially in roles that need availability.

Conclusion: Choose What You Miss

JOMO is not about doing less for its own sake. It is about noticing that every yes costs something, and picking what you pay for. Try one small no this week, whether an event, an app, or a group chat, and notice how it feels the next day. If it feels like relief, you are probably practicing JOMO. If it feels like hiding, take that seriously and talk to someone you trust.
