# Social Media Detox: How to Do One, What to Expect and a 7-Day Plan

If you have been thinking about taking a social media break, or even quitting social media altogether, a social media detox is the easiest way to test what you would actually miss. It has an end date, so it is a trial rather than a vow. This guide covers what a detox is, what the research does and does not show, how long to go for and a simple 7-day plan, plus a way back that keeps the benefits.

## What is a social media detox?

A social media detox is a deliberate break from social media for a set period, usually a few days to a few weeks. You can step away from every platform or only the ones that drain you, but it works best with a start date, an end date and a plan for what replaces the scrolling.

It differs from quitting for good. Because a detox is temporary, it is easier to commit to, and it shows you what the habit was doing for you before you decide whether to quit social media permanently.

## Signs you may need a social media detox

Pew Research Center's 2025 survey of 5,022 US adults found that 84% use YouTube, 71% use Facebook and half use Instagram, so heavy use is ordinary. These signs suggest it may be costing you something:

- You reach for your phone without deciding to, sometimes without noticing.
- You open one app, close it and open another within seconds.
- You often feel worse after scrolling, whether that is envy, irritation or flatness.
- It eats time you meant to spend on sleep, work or people.
- You check for likes or replies and feel it when they are slow.

One or two of these is common. If most apply on most days, a break is worth trying.

## What the research says about a social media break

The strongest evidence comes from randomised experiments, where people are assigned to take a break or carry on as usual. Surveys alone only show that heavy users tend to feel worse, not which came first.

| Study | Who and how long | What was found | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lambert et al., 2022 (University of Bath), Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking | 154 adults aged 18 to 72 who used social media daily; one week off Facebook, Instagram, Twitter and TikTok | Better well-being and lower depression and anxiety than a group that carried on | One week; self-reported; no long-term follow-up |
| Allcott et al., 2020, American Economic Review | 2,743 US Facebook users; some deactivated for four weeks | Small but significant rise in subjective well-being; more offline time with family and friends; less news knowledge | Facebook only; volunteers recruited through Facebook ads |
| Hunt et al., 2018 (University of Pennsylvania), Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology | 143 undergraduates; three weeks capped at 10 minutes per app per day (Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat) | Less loneliness and depression than the control group; both groups reported less anxiety and fear of missing out | Students only; a limit, not a full break |
| Allcott et al., 2025 (NBER working paper) | Large experiments before the 2020 US election; six weeks off Facebook or Instagram | Small gains on an index of happiness, depression and anxiety (0.060 and 0.041 standard deviations) | Working paper; effects are small |

Read together, these experiments point the same way: stepping back tended to give a modest lift to mood or well-being. The limits matter, though:

- Most outcomes are self-reported, and participants knew they were in a test.
- Follow-ups are short. The Bath team said it wanted to test whether benefits last, so one week's result should not be read as permanent.
- Samples are narrow: students in one study, Facebook users in another.
- Averages hide individuals. In the 2025 working paper's exploratory analysis, the Facebook effect appeared to be driven by people over 35 and the Instagram effect by women under 25. The 2020 study also found less news knowledge after deactivation, so a break has a trade-off.
- Social media is not harmful by default. The American Psychological Association's 2023 advisory on adolescents says effects depend on the person, their circumstances and what they see.

In short, a break is a reasonable experiment with a plausible upside, not a proven treatment.

## How long should a social media detox last?

No study has found an ideal length. The experiments above tested one to six weeks, and the Bath trial found differences after just one. A week is a sensible minimum for a social media break you want to learn from. Pick the version you will actually finish:

- **48 hours:** a weekend dry run if a week feels too big.
- **7 days:** long enough to get past the restless first days and spot a pattern.
- **30 days:** extend only after a week-long trial, with weekly check-ins.

If you cannot step away completely because work or family run through social platforms, a limit is a fair alternative. The Penn study capped each app at 10 minutes a day.

## A 7-day social media detox plan

Start on a day with nothing high-stakes. This plan assumes you remove the apps but keep your accounts, so you can return on purpose.

- **Day 1: Set up.** Note your current screen time, write down why you are doing this and tell two or three people to text or call you instead. Log out and delete the apps. Your phone's own tools can help; see [the iPhone settings worth changing](/article/iphone-settings-change-now-2026) or [hidden Android features](/article/hidden-android-features-2026) for downtime and app limits.
- **Day 2: Notice the pull.** Expect reflex reaching for the phone. Each time, jot down what set it off: boredom, a queue, stress, a lull at work.
- **Day 3: Fill the gaps.** Choose one replacement for each of your top three triggers (ideas below).
- **Day 4: Watch for swaps.** Check you are not just moving the scrolling to YouTube, news or shopping apps. If you are, apply the same rules there.
- **Day 5: Reach out directly.** Message or call one person you usually only see through their posts.
- **Day 6: Protect your sleep.** Charge your phone outside the bedroom and use a basic alarm clock.
- **Day 7: Review.** Rate your mood, sleep and focus from 1 to 10 and compare with Day 1. Decide whether to extend, return with rules or stay away.

## What to do instead of social media

Replace the trigger, not just the app. A few options:

- **Waiting or commuting:** an audiobook, a podcast or a paperback.
- **Feeling lonely:** text a friend, or try a small hobby forum; see [tiny internet communities](/article/tiny-internet-communities-2026).
- **Wanting the news:** pick one newsletter or one set time to check.
- **Evenings:** a walk, cooking, a game with someone in the room, a creative project.
- **A restless phone habit:** simpler gear can help, as in our look at [the dumb tech trend](/article/dumb-tech-trend-2026).

## What people commonly experience

Experiences vary, and the research does not promise any particular result. Common reports include:

- Restlessness and reflex checking in the first days, like patting a pocket for something that is missing.
- Boredom, followed for some by more time for other things.
- Missing particular people or updates. Participants in the 2020 Facebook study said they missed it while off it.
- Relief and better sleep for some people, and little change for others.

You may read that a detox "resets your brain". The experiments above measured mood, loneliness and time use, not brain changes, so treat that as a figure of speech. For the attention question, see [TikTok and attention span](/article/tiktok-brain-attention-span-2026).

A detox is not a treatment. If low mood or anxiety is persistent, or is getting in the way of daily life, speak to a GP, therapist or other qualified professional.

## How to come back without sliding into old habits

Most relapses happen when people return to everything at once. Instead:

1. Return to one platform, the one you missed most.
2. Keep apps off your home screen, or use the browser only, so opening takes effort.
3. Set a window and a timer. Ten minutes per app is the Penn study's cap, a starting point rather than a rule.
4. Turn off notifications except direct messages.
5. Mute or unfollow accounts that left you feeling worse.
6. Check in after two weeks: has your Day 7 rating slipped?

A slip is information, not failure. For a wider approach, read our guide to [digital detox and JOMO](/article/digital-detox-jomo-2026).

## Deactivate, delete or limit: which should you choose?

| Option | What it does | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delete the apps only | Removes the app; account and data stay | A first, low-commitment detox | Easy to reinstall, and you can still log in by browser, so log out too |
| Deactivate the account | Hides your profile; usually reversible by logging back in | A full break, the method used in the Facebook studies | Rules differ by platform, and logging in can reactivate it |
| Limit your use | Timers, one app only or set windows | People who need social media for work or family | Takes follow-through; limits are easy to dismiss |
| Delete the account | Permanent removal | Only if you are sure | Download your data first; deletion rules and grace periods vary |

## FAQ

### What is a social media detox?

A deliberate, time-limited break from social media, from a weekend to several weeks. You choose which platforms to drop, how long to stay away and what to do instead.

### How long should a social media detox last?

No ideal length has been established. Experiments tested one to six weeks, and one week produced measurable differences in the Bath trial, so seven days is a sensible start.

### What are the benefits of quitting social media?

In the experiments above, people reported modest gains in well-being, and some reported less depression, anxiety or loneliness, plus more offline time. Results vary, and you may also lose some news knowledge or contact.

### What happens to your brain and mood when you quit social media?

The studies measured self-reported mood, loneliness and time use, not brain activity. Many people feel restless at first; some then feel calmer, while others notice little change.

### How do I do a detox without losing touch?

Tell close contacts how to reach you by text or call, move key group chats to a messaging app and message people directly during the week.

### Should I deactivate my accounts or just delete the apps?

Deleting the apps is the gentler first step, but log out as well because the browser still works. Deactivating is a firmer break. Choose based on how easily you slip back.

### How do I come back without relapsing?

Return to one platform at a time, keep apps off your home screen, set a timer, turn off notifications and review how you feel after two weeks.

## Conclusion

A social media detox is a low-cost experiment: a week off, a plan and an honest review. The research suggests a modest, uneven upside, so go in curious rather than expecting a transformation. Start with the 7-day plan, note what you miss and what you do not, then decide how much room social media deserves in your life.
